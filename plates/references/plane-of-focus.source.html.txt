<h2>Where a photo turns sharp</h2>
<p>A lens can only bring <b>one distance</b> to a perfect point. Everything at that distance forms a thin sheet in the world: the <b>plane of focus</b>. Here it is the glowing sheet sweeping through the valley, and the bright line where it cuts the trees, the cabin and the mountains.</p>
<h3>Why the rest goes soft</h3>
<p>Light from any point spreads out, passes through the whole opening of the lens and is bent back together. From a point <b>on</b> the plane, the rays meet exactly on the image plane: a point. From anything nearer or farther they meet in front of it or behind it, so they arrive as a small disc, the <b>circle of confusion</b>. That disc is the blur. The rings on the glass at the back show how big it is for the tree, the cabin and the peak.</p>
<h3>The focus ring</h3>
<p>Turning the ring slides the glass forward or back inside the barrel. Closer subjects focus farther behind the lens, so the glass moves out; far ones focus closer to it, so it moves in. Drag the ring, the slider on the console or the one in the card.</p>
<h3>The aperture</h3>
<p>The iris is the adjustable hole in the middle of the lens. Wide open (<b>f/2</b>) the cone of light is fat, the discs are big and only a thin slice is sharp. Stopped down (<b>f/16</b>) the cone is thin, every disc shrinks, and the sharp zone grows deep. That is why landscapes are shot at f/11 and portraits at f/2.</p>
<h3>Upside down?</h3>
<p>Yes. Every lens projects the world upside down, and it is drawn that way on the glass. The screens on the console flip it back, the way your camera does.</p>
