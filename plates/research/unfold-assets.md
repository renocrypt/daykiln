# UNFOLD: asset research

Researched on 2026-09-23 (finished 2026-09-24). Catalogue only; nothing downloaded.

> **2026-09-24:** The Role column records license status from this first pass. It does not say what is picked: selection is now beauty-first, and current picks are in [Selection in DIRECTIONS.md](../DIRECTIONS.md#selection).

Scope: the UNFOLD needs in [DIRECTIONS.md](../DIRECTIONS.md#assets-to-source). Every source below was opened during this research; some GitHub, Wikimedia Commons, Library of Congress, and archive records were read through their public APIs. Licences are quoted from the holding institution or publisher. "Not stated" means the source did not say.

## Top picks

1. **Coastline, land, graticule:** Natural Earth at 1:110m, 1:50m, and 1:10m. It is public domain, and its three matched scales form a ready level-of-detail ladder. For relief, Tom Patterson's hand-drawn *Manual Shaded Relief of the World* (10,800 × 5,400 px, public domain) reads as engraving, not satellite data, and sits well on warm paper.
2. **Projection library:** d3-geo-polygon, with d3-geo-projection and d3-geo underneath (all ISC). It has Airocean, butterfly, and a generic polyhedral builder whose face tree supplies the hinges for unfolding. It is the only one of the three under active maintenance. Tissot values must be computed in-house.
3. **Historical gores:** Hondius's terrestrial gores of 1615 at the Library of Congress (9,318 × 4,384 px, "free to use and reuse"). Uncoloured copperplate on cream paper is the UNFOLD palette already. For Waldseemüller, use LOC's 1879 facsimile (5,984 × 7,773 px) and the LMU Munich sheet (Public Domain Mark), the only open scan of an authentic copy.
4. **Antique globes and stands:** Blaeu's terrestrial globe of 1645–1648 at the Rijksmuseum (public domain, 5,829 × 7,110 px). One clean studio view shows the meridian ring, hour circle, horizon ring, and columned stand. The Met's Blaeu celestial globe adds a second view and a written description of the ring construction.
5. **Dymaxion reference:** the 1954 Fuller and Sadao *Raleigh Edition* at the American Geographical Society Library (14,172 × 9,314 px), reference only. Render the projection with `geoAirocean` rather than ship any Fuller image.

Distortion: no maintained JavaScript package computing Tissot's *h*, *k*, and *ω* was found. Compute them from a central-difference Jacobian of d3's raw projections (about 20 lines) and check a few points against PROJ. On a free-cut mesh, compute the same per triangle from the flattening's Jacobian.

Gores: d3's `geoInterrupt` with `geoPolyconicRaw` or `geoRectangularPolyconicRaw` covers the classic 12 or 24 gores. [JoshData/globe-gores](https://github.com/JoshData/globe-gores) shows another method but carries no license, so it is reference only.

## Fact checks

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence and source |
|---|---|---|
| Waldseemüller's 1507 gores are "among the earliest uses of the name America." | Confirmed; can be stated more precisely | The gores bear the name: the large southern land "is named 'America'" ([Bell Library](https://apps.lib.umn.edu/bell/map/WALD/GLOBE/new.html)); they "also apply the name 'America' to the New World" ([Van Duzer](https://razoncartografica.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/van-duzer-waldseemuller-globe-gores-munich-english.pdf)). LOC: Waldseemüller "created what are thought to be the first set of printed globe gores in 1507," and the gores "along with his wall map also created in 1507 were also where the name America first appears on a map" ([LOC blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/maps/2025/03/globe-gores/)). The wall map record reads "First document known to name America" ([LOC 2003626426](https://www.loc.gov/item/2003626426/)). The *Cosmographiae introductio* (1507) explains the name. Two cautions. The printing dates of surviving copies are debated: the LMU copy is "nach 1507," and the Bell Library believes LOC's wall map is "a later edition of ca. 1515" ([Bell](https://apps.lib.umn.edu/bell/WaldsWebsite/AtTheBell.htm)). The Munich BSB copy is a forgery and must not be cited or shown. Suggested wording: "Martin Waldseemüller's printed globe gores of 1507, which with his wall map of the same year are the first known maps to use the name America." |
| Surviving copies of the gores. | Confirmed: four authentic | Minnesota (James Ford Bell Library), Offenburg, a private collection (London auction, 2005), and LMU Munich (a later printing). The Bayerische Staatsbibliothek copy is a forgery (BSB, 2018), and the 2017 Christie's copy is suspected. | Africa is "about fourteen times" larger than Greenland. | Confirmed | Africa: "approximately 11,724,000 square miles (30,365,000 square km)" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Africa)). Greenland: 2,166,086 km² ([Wikipedia infobox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland); the CIA World Factbook page now redirects to a farewell notice). Ratio 14.0. Greenland's area includes its ice sheet, which covers "more than four-fifths" of it ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greenland)). On Mercator, area scale is sec²φ, so near Greenland's 70–75°N land is inflated roughly 9–15 times while equatorial Africa is barely enlarged; this is our computation, not a quoted source. |
| "A sphere cannot be flattened without tearing or stretching (Gauss, *Theorema Egregium*, 1827)." | Corrected | Gauss proved the theorem in 1827 (published 1828 in *Commentationes Societatis Regiae Scientiarum Gottingensis Recentiores*, vol. 6): Gaussian curvature is intrinsic. The sphere has curvature 1/R² and the plane 0, so they are "not isometric, even locally," and "no planar (flat) map of Earth can be perfect, even for a portion of the Earth's surface" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_Egregium); [Gauss bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss)). Two corrections follow. First, tearing does not avoid distortion: even the smallest gore must stretch, so cuts only reduce and redistribute it. The doc's "tearing or stretching" implies tearing alone would do. Second, the impossibility itself is older: "The geometers from ancient Greece knew that it is not possible to have a faithful representation of (part of) a sphere on a Euclidean plane" ([Papadopoulos 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.11245)). Gauss supplied the general reason, not the first proof. Suggested wording: "No piece of a sphere, however small, can be laid flat without stretching; cutting only moves the distortion around. Gauss's *Theorema Egregium* (1827) gives the reason: curvature belongs to the surface itself." The doc's "preserve shape and area fails; preserve area and shape distorts" is sound if stated as: a map may preserve local angles or areas, never both, and never all distances. |
| Tissot's indicatrix is "a field of small circles that the projection stretches into ellipses." | Corrected (minor) | Tissot (1859 and 1871) showed that "a circle of infinitesimal radius" on the globe maps to "an ellipse whose axes indicate the two principal directions along which scale is maximal and minimal." On conformal maps the indicatrices "are all circles of size varying by location"; on equal-area maps they "all have the same area, though their shapes and orientations vary" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissot%27s_indicatrix)). So not every projection stretches the circles into ellipses. Suggested wording: "Tissot's indicatrix: identical tiny circles on the globe, each drawn as the shape the projection turns it into. Conformal maps keep them round but change their size; equal-area maps keep their area but squash them." For truthfulness, compute each ellipse from the projection's local derivatives. Projecting a finite circle, as d3's own example does ([Observable](https://observablehq.com/@d3/tissots-indicatrix)), is only an approximation. |
| Fuller's Dymaxion map "unfolds the Earth onto a polyhedron." | Corrected (minor) | The 1943 *Life* "Dymaxion World" used a cuboctahedron; Fuller's 1946 patent covered it and "expired in 1963" ([Huffman](https://somethingaboutmaps.wordpress.com/2022/11/14/the-unnecessary-peril-of-the-fuller-projection/)). The 1954 map with Shoji Sadao is icosahedral ("Edges of icosa triangles…", [AGSL record](https://collections.lib.uwm.edu/digital/collection/agdm/id/14899/)). Suggested wording: "projects the Earth onto a polyhedron (a cuboctahedron in 1943, an icosahedron in 1954) and unfolds it flat." BFI claims "Dymaxion" and "Fuller Projection Map" as trademarks ([BFI](https://www.bfi.org/about-fuller/big-ideas/dymaxion-map/)). |

## Gaps

- **No open scan of the Minnesota copy.** It is an authentic 1507 sheet, and probably the original behind LOC's 1879 facsimile (that sheet came to LOC in the Hauslab-Liechtenstein accession), but UMedia has no record. Ask the James Ford Bell Library for a high-resolution file and its terms. The Offenburg and private copies have no open scans either.
- **Coronelli 1688 gores, State Library of New South Wales.** This is the most beautiful set found. Rumsey's copy is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, which makes it reference only. SLNSW's own catalogue status could not be read because the catalogue served a human-verification page. Someone should check it by hand; if SLNSW states no restrictions, this set becomes the top gore candidate.
- **Commercial or not.** BnF Gallica allows free non-commercial reuse and charges for commercial reuse. Decide whether Chroma counts as non-commercial before using any Gallica image.
- **Natural Earth 6.0 licence.** The preliminary data page states no licence. Confirm with the authors before shipping it; Natural Earth 4.x/5.x is safe in the meantime.
- **GSHHG licence.** LGPL-3.0 for data needs a decision. It is not needed if Natural Earth suffices at macro zoom.
- **Multi-view brass reference.** The open-licence globes offer one or two views each. Royal Museums Greenwich has several views of a great Coronelli floor globe but no open licence. No open macro photographs of engraved degree scales on a meridian ring were found; shoot or model them.
- **Missing sizes.** Pixel dimensions are not stated for the Met images, the Rumsey single gores, and the Gallica original. The LMU PDF's embedded resolution is known only through a Commons extraction.
- **Tissot computation.** No maintained JavaScript library computes *h*, *k*, and *ω*. Plan a small in-house module, checked against PROJ.
- **Dymaxion naming.** Whether public copy may say "Dymaxion" or "Fuller Projection" is a trademark question for BFI or counsel.
- **Greenland area source.** The CIA World Factbook, the usual reference, is no longer online. The figure here comes from Wikipedia; Statistics Greenland would be a better citation before public use.
